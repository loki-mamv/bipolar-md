Does Marijuana Make Bipolar Disorder Worse?

November 26, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: For most people with bipolar disorder, the evidence says yes. A 2015 meta-analysis in the Journal of Affective Disorders found that cannabis use is linked to worse manic symptoms in people already diagnosed. Continued use after a manic episode is linked to more relapse. These studies show association, not proven cause, and CBD alone is a separate, less studied question.

What do studies show about cannabis and manic symptoms?

The clearest signal is for mania. A 2015 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled six prospective studies covering 2,391 people who had experienced manic symptoms. The studies supported a link between cannabis use and worse manic symptoms in people who already had bipolar disorder.

The same review pooled two studies on new cases. Cannabis use was linked to about a 3-fold higher risk of new manic symptoms (odds ratio 2.97). The authors called their findings tentative because the studies were few and of mixed quality.

The National Institute on Drug Abuse notes that THC is the compound that changes mood, thoughts, and perception. It also reports that THC potency in seized cannabis rose from 3.96% in 1995 to 16.14% in 2022. Dispensary flower and concentrates can exceed 40%. Most of the older bipolar studies were done when products were weaker.

Does using cannabis make relapse more likely if you already have bipolar disorder?

Several cohort studies point the same way, though not all of them agree. Here are the main numbers, side by side:

  • Relapse after a manic or mixed episode. The EMBLEM study followed 1,922 adults for two years after a manic or mixed episode. Those who kept using cannabis had lower remission and recovery rates, higher recurrence, and more work impairment than people who never used.
  • Quitting helped. In the same study, people who stopped cannabis early in follow-up did as well as never-users. They had the highest remission rate of any group (68.1%) and the lowest relapse rate (29.8%).
  • Early rehospitalization. A 2026 French cohort of 200 young adults (ages 18 to 29) with bipolar or psychotic disorders found that cannabis use at admission was linked to 2.8 times the risk of readmission in the first month after discharge. The risk was lower by 3 months and no longer significant by 6 months.
  • A mixed result. A 2025 Barcelona cohort of 279 patients admitted for mania found cannabis was not linked to a higher relapse rate. It was linked to earlier illness onset, psychotic symptoms, and a higher chance of stopping treatment.

That last point matters. If cannabis makes people more likely to stop their medication, part of its effect on relapse may run through missed treatment. Our guide to rapid cycling bipolar disorder covers how substances fit into an unstable course.

Can cannabis cause bipolar disorder in someone who did not have it?

Large registry studies show a strong link, but they cannot prove cause. A 2023 Danish study in JAMA Psychiatry followed more than 6.6 million people. A diagnosis of cannabis use disorder was linked to a higher risk of later bipolar disorder: hazard ratio 2.96 in men and 2.54 in women. The link was stronger for bipolar disorder with psychotic features (hazard ratio 4.05).

A 2026 Kaiser Permanente cohort of 463,396 teenagers found that past-year cannabis use at ages 13 to 17 was linked to about twice the risk of a later bipolar diagnosis (adjusted hazard ratio 2.01). After the researchers also adjusted for earlier psychiatric conditions, the figure was 1.73.

The Danish study also found a higher risk of depression after cannabis use disorder. If low mood is the bigger issue for you or a family member, depression.md covers that side.

Why can't researchers say cannabis directly causes worse bipolar disorder?

Every study above is observational. No one can ethically assign people with bipolar disorder to use THC for years. That leaves several explanations open:

  1. Self-treatment. Some people may use cannabis to cope with early symptoms, so the illness may come first.
  2. Shared risk factors. A 2019 meta-analysis of 53 studies found cannabis use in about 24% of people with bipolar disorder. Users were more often young, male, and single, and more often used tobacco, alcohol, and other drugs.
  3. Missed treatment. As the Barcelona cohort showed, cannabis users may stop medication more often.
  4. Measurement gaps. Most studies record "use" or "use disorder." Few record THC strength, amount, or how often.

Even so, the direction is consistent. The EMBLEM finding that quitters did as well as never-users fits the idea that cannabis itself plays a role.

Is CBD different from THC for bipolar disorder?

CBD does not produce a high, and the evidence on it is thin. The FDA states it has approved only one CBD product, a prescription drug for seizures in Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, Dravet syndrome, or tuberous sclerosis complex. No CBD product is approved for any mood disorder.

  • Bipolar depression. A 2024 pilot trial of 35 people found adjunct CBD did no better than placebo on depression scores at week 8. It also did not raise manic symptoms compared with placebo.
  • Mania. A 2010 report of two inpatients found CBD was not effective for a manic episode.
  • The big picture. A 2026 Lancet Psychiatry meta-analysis of 54 cannabinoid trials found too little data to analyze bipolar disorder at all.

The Epidiolex label lists liver enzyme elevations, sleepiness, and suicidal thoughts as warnings. It also states that taking valproate with higher Epidiolex doses raises the risk of liver enzyme elevations. Valproate (Depakote) is a common bipolar medicine. The FDA notes that store-bought CBD can also affect other medicines and is not monitored the way a prescription is.

What should you tell your psychiatrist about cannabis use?

Honest numbers help your care team more than a yes or no. Before your next visit, write down:

  1. What you use: flower, vapes, edibles, concentrates, or CBD-only products.
  2. The THC percentage on the label, if you know it.
  3. How many days a week you use, and when you started.
  4. Any link you have noticed between use and sleep, energy, or mood shifts.
  5. Whether you have skipped medication on days you used.
  6. Any CBD product and its label, so your prescriber can check it against your medicines.

The same logic applies to alcohol, which our guide to alcohol and mood stabilizers covers. For a wider list of what sets off episodes, see bipolar episode triggers.

The bottom line

The research does not prove that cannabis causes bipolar disorder. It does consistently link THC use to more manic symptoms, more relapse, and earlier onset. The most hopeful finding is that people who stopped after a manic episode did about as well as people who never used. CBD alone is not well studied in bipolar disorder and is not approved to treat it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can smoking weed trigger a manic episode?

Studies link cannabis use to the onset and worsening of manic symptoms. A 2015 meta-analysis found about a 3-fold higher risk of new manic symptoms in users. These studies show a link, not proof that cannabis alone set off a given episode.

Is it safe to use CBD oil if I have bipolar disorder?

There is not enough research to call it safe or effective for bipolar disorder. The FDA warns that CBD can affect liver enzymes and other medicines. The Epidiolex label says valproate plus higher CBD doses raises liver enzyme risk, so ask your prescriber first.

Will quitting cannabis improve my bipolar symptoms?

In the EMBLEM study, people who stopped using cannabis after a manic or mixed episode had outcomes similar to people who never used. People who kept using had more recurrence and worse work function. Your own results depend on your treatment and other factors.

Does cannabis help with bipolar depression?

No trial has shown that it does. A 2024 pilot trial found CBD was no better than placebo for bipolar depression at week 8. No trial has tested THC as a bipolar depression treatment.

Are edibles safer than smoking for people with bipolar disorder?

No study has compared them in bipolar disorder. NIDA notes that edibles can deliver large amounts of THC, and THC is the compound linked to mood and perception changes. The form matters less than how much THC you take in.
