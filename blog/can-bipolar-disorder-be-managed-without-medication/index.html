Can Bipolar Disorder Be Managed Without Medication?

October 20, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida
Table of Contents

The short answer: For most people with bipolar disorder, especially bipolar I, the evidence does not support managing it without medication. Major guidelines treat medication as the base of long-term care. Therapy, sleep regularity, and avoiding alcohol and drugs clearly help, but the trials tested them alongside medication, not instead of it. A small amount of evidence suggests some people with bipolar II depression improve with therapy alone.

What do treatment guidelines say about medication?

Both major guidelines put medication first for long-term care. The UK's NICE guideline CG185 says to offer lithium as the first-line, long-term medication for bipolar disorder. It calls lithium "the most effective long-term treatment for bipolar disorder." If lithium does not work or does not suit the person, NICE suggests an antipsychotic instead.

The 2018 CANMAT and ISBD guidelines say the same thing in more detail. For maintenance in bipolar I, they list lithium, quetiapine, divalproex, lamotrigine, asenapine, and aripiprazole as first-line options. They also say medications that worked during an acute episode should generally continue into the maintenance phase.

The U.S. National Institute of Mental Health notes that bipolar disorder "usually requires lifelong treatment and does not go away on its own." Symptoms can fade for months or years. That quiet stretch is often the treatment working, not the illness ending.

Can therapy replace medication for bipolar disorder?

Therapy adds real benefit, but the strongest evidence comes from studies where everyone also took medication. The largest review is a 2021 network meta-analysis in JAMA Psychiatry. It pooled 39 randomized trials with 3,863 participants. Every trial compared medication plus a structured therapy with medication plus usual care.

The findings were encouraging:

  • Structured therapy was linked to lower recurrence rates than control treatment (odds ratio 0.56).
  • Psychoeducation with practiced illness-management skills worked better in family or group formats than one-on-one.
  • Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) helped stabilize depressive symptoms. Family therapy and interpersonal therapy showed similar trends with less certainty.

The authors concluded that outpatients "may benefit from skills-based psychosocial interventions combined with pharmacotherapy." Note the last two words. The study cannot tell us how therapy performs without medication, because no trial tested that.

NICE agrees on the role of therapy. It recommends a structured psychological intervention designed for bipolar disorder to prevent relapse. It also recommends family intervention for people who live with or stay close to family. Our article on therapy for bipolar disorder covers each approach in depth.

What happens when people stop their mood stabilizer?

Relapse is common, and it often comes quickly. A 1991 analysis of 14 studies looked at 257 people with bipolar I who stopped lithium after an average of 30 months. More than half of new episodes came within 10 weeks. Half of the group had relapsed by 5.0 months. The authors said the early risk, especially of mania, may exceed what the untreated illness would predict.

Feeling well does not protect against this. In a 1993 follow-up study of 64 people stable on lithium for 18 to 120 months, 67% chose to stop because they had been well for a long time. Within 5 years, 75% had a new episode.

How you stop matters a great deal. A pooled study of 161 patients compared stopping lithium over 1 to 14 days with stopping over 15 to 30 days. Median time to relapse was 4.0 months after rapid stopping and 20.0 months after gradual stopping. NICE advises reducing lithium gradually over at least 4 weeks, and preferably up to 3 months. It also advises close monitoring for 3 months afterward. Read more in what happens if you stop taking lithium.

Does anyone do well without medication?

Some people do, but no one can reliably predict who in advance. In the same 161-patient study, 37% of people who stopped gradually stayed well for 3 years off lithium. Only 1.8% of those who stopped rapidly did. So a minority stays stable, and a slow taper improves the odds.

Bipolar II depression is the area with the most direct evidence for therapy alone. A 2018 randomized trial gave 92 unmedicated adults with bipolar II depression weekly Interpersonal and Social Rhythm Therapy (IPSRT). Half also got quetiapine and half got a placebo pill. The quetiapine group improved faster, but it also gained more weight and had more side effects. Response rates were similar, at 67.4% across the whole sample. The authors cautioned that the study had no inactive comparison group.

This is one trial in one subtype. It does not show that bipolar I can be managed without medication. Mania can cause psychosis, risky decisions, and hospital stays, so the stakes of a relapse are higher there.

Which daily habits help keep mood stable?

Habits do not replace treatment, but they make it work better. NIMH lists several self-care steps:

  • Keep a regular routine for eating, sleeping, and exercise.
  • Avoid drugs and alcohol.
  • Keep a life chart or mood journal to spot mood swings early.
  • Keep medical and therapy appointments, and take medication as directed.

Sleep deserves special attention. IPSRT is built around stable daily rhythms, because disrupted sleep and schedules can set off episodes. See bipolar disorder and sleep for practical steps. NICE also asks clinicians to discuss alcohol, tobacco, and drug use, since these can interfere with both medication and therapy.

Depressive episodes are often the longest part of the illness. If low mood is your main struggle, depression.md covers depressive symptoms and treatment in more detail.

How do you talk to a prescriber about wanting less medication?

Wanting fewer pills is common and reasonable. Side effects such as weight gain, sedation, or tremor are real reasons people want change. NICE says clinicians should discuss the benefits and risks of stopping medication, including for women who may want to become pregnant.

Some steps that make the conversation productive:

  1. Name the specific problem, such as weight gain, fog, or a pregnancy plan.
  2. Ask whether a different medication or a lower dose could solve it.
  3. Ask to add a structured therapy, such as CBT, family-focused therapy, or IPSRT.
  4. If you both decide to taper, agree on a slow schedule and a relapse plan.

NIMH advises people to avoid stopping medication without talking to a health care provider first. If you have already stopped and notice early signs of mania or depression, contact your prescriber soon. If you have thoughts of suicide, call or text 988.

The bottom line

For bipolar I, medication remains the base of long-term care, and stopping it carries a high relapse risk, especially when done quickly. Therapy, regular sleep, and avoiding alcohol and drugs all lower risk when added to medication. A small body of research suggests some people with bipolar II depression respond to therapy alone. Any change should be planned with a prescriber, tapered slowly, and monitored closely.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it possible to be off bipolar medication for years without an episode?

Yes, some people stay well for years after stopping, but most do not. In one pooled study, 37% of people who tapered lithium gradually stayed well for 3 years, compared with 1.8% of those who stopped quickly. There is no reliable test to predict who will stay well.

Does lithium cause withdrawal symptoms when you stop it?

The main risk after stopping lithium is a return of mania or depression, often within weeks to months. Studies show this risk is higher after rapid stopping than after a gradual taper. NICE advises tapering over at least 4 weeks and monitoring for 3 months afterward.

Can natural supplements replace mood stabilizers?

No major guideline recommends any supplement as a replacement for mood stabilizers in bipolar disorder. Some supplements also interact with prescription drugs or can affect mood. Tell your prescriber about anything you take.

Is bipolar II easier to manage without medication than bipolar I?

Bipolar II has more research on therapy alone than bipolar I. A 2018 trial found many people with bipolar II depression improved with IPSRT plus placebo, though improvement was faster with added quetiapine. Bipolar I carries the risk of full mania, so guidelines rely more firmly on medication there.

What type of therapy works best alongside medication?

A 2021 review of 39 trials found that structured therapies lowered recurrence when added to medication. Psychoeducation in family or group formats did especially well, and CBT helped with depressive symptoms. Family-focused therapy and IPSRT are other options with trial support.
